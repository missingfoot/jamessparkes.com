**The Printing Press**

Whoever owned the presses was powerful and they could influence the way that the world was understood. Each media technology saw a period of time when its powers were witnessed, followed by a period of consolidation and shrinkage.

This resulted in institutionalisation (such as governments or corporations), standardisation, censorship and control. That then gave way for reliable distribution from a few sources, i.e., it went from many dispersed media outlets to a few large outlets.

While many presses still remain a distinction is made between publisher and printer. There are many printers but few reliable publishers whose output is stocked in reputable bookstores.

**An Assurance of Quality?**

The role of the editor becomes important:

*   Book Editor
*   Picture Editor
*   Copywriter
*   Curator
*   Broadcast Editor

The editor selects the content to be distributed and the audience is assured quality.

*Creators > Gate Keeper > Audience*

**Gutenberg Economics**

This process of mass media controlled by gatekeepers has been described as Gutenberg Economics. It is arguably the commercial model that underpins mass media from Gutenberg onwards and allowed the ruling classes to impose some control over the media and its audiences while still allowing creative output.

What are the benefits of the Gutenberg Economics model?

*   To the audience?
*   To the gatekeepers?
*   To the creative?
*   To the status quo?

What are the drawbacks of the Gutenberg Economics model?

*   To the audience?
*   To the gatekeepers?
*   To the creative?
*   To the status quo?

Gutenberg Economics have dictated the ways in which media is produced and consumed globally over the last 500 years. A select few voices have been communicated to a wide audience and so neither the audience or the voices discarded have a similar opportunity to be heard.

This has generated profit which has allowed the structures to develop and has determined what media is produced for the audience, promising quality, reliability, ease of accessibility. This model also actively discourages competition, incorporating any new occurrences that show innovation.

Despite this model something appears to be changing. Is it the end of Gutenberg Economics and the rise of Free?

**The Internet**

The internet has only really had a major impact on the average individual over the past decade. It is difficult to identify specific changes that have occurred as a result of the rise of the internet as during the same time-frame we have seen a great number of major social, economic and political changes.

How might they have impacted?

*   Fall of the Soviet Union and the end of the Cold War
*   A general move towards Free Market Economics
*   A move towards consolidation in the media sector
*   Longer working hours / overtime culture in the UK
*   Mobile phone proliferation
*   The longest period of economic growth (1997 to 2007) in the UK

What was life like before?

*   How did people purchase things? Book Shops? Music Shops? Electrical Shops?
*   How much choice was there?
*   How did you select an item?

What was life like before?

*   What about your entertainment?
*   What about your news?

Wider Issues

*   How empowered was the buyer?
*   How much control did the seller have?
*   How did you know you were getting a good deal?
*   Would you buy a no-name brand?

There is now a staggering number of national and international websites, often general in nature. The offer a huge selection of stock, but customers have to decide what they want to purchase. Prices and margins vary enormously but are generally lower, set by demand and competition.

People can now use websites and read buyer reviews for advice. Outside of returns, people tend to sell things through eBay, where a clear item value can be found. We’ve come to rely on a few large internet companies such as:

*   [Amazon](http://www.amazon.com/)

In the same way that shopping has changed so too has our relationship with media.

**Clay Shirky**

In 1993, the Knight Ridder newspaper chain began investigating piracy of Dave Barry’s popular column, which was published by the Miami Herald and syndicated widely.

Some of their results concluded that a 2000-person strong mailing list was reading pirated versions of the column and that a teenager in the Midwest did some of the copying himself, because he loved Barry’s work so much he wanted everybody to be able to read it.

> “When a 14 year old kid can blow up your business in his spare time, not because he hates you but because he loves you, then you got a problem.”

**Potential Solutions**

*   Partner with companies like America Online—a subscription based edited version of the open internet
*   Educate the public about the behaviours required of them by copyright law
*   New payment models such as micropayments were proposed
*   Pursue the profit margins enjoyed by radio and TV—if they became purely ad-supported
*   Convince tech firms to make their hardware and software less capable of sharing—or to partner with the businesses running data networks to achieve the same goal
*   Then there was the nuclear option: sue copyright infringers directly—making an example of them

However, the unthinkable scenario unfolded to something like this:

*   The ability to share content wouldn’t shrink—it would grow
*   Walled gardens would prove unpopular
*   Digital advertising reduced inefficiencies and profits
*   Dislike of micropayments would prevent widespread use
*   People would resist being educated
*   Old habits of advertisers and readers would not transfer online
*   Litigation would fail to stop massive—sustained law-breaking
*   Hardware and software vendors did not care for IP
*   DRM’s requirement of decoding made it hackable

Here’s how we’re going to preserve the old forms of organisation in a world of cheap perfect copies: the core assumption behind all imagined outcomes was that the organisational form of the newspaper and Gutenberg Economics as a general-purpose vehicle for publishing a variety of news and opinion, was basically sound, and only needed a digital facelift, e.g., The Wall Street Journal’s paywall solution.

When we talk about newspapers, we talk about them being critical for informing the public; we never say they’re critical for informing their customers. We assume that the value of the news ramifies outwards from the readership to society as a whole.

Murdoch is signing up to prevent that value from escaping. He wants to only inform his customers, he doesn’t want his stories to be shared and circulated widely. In fact, his ability to charge for the paywall is going to come down to his ability to lock the public out of the conversation convened by the Times.

*Clay Shirky, July 2010*

**If the old model is broken, what will work in its place?**

Nothing. Nothing will work. There is no general model for newspapers to replace the one the internet just broke. Does that mean that this is the end of Gutenberg Economics?

It makes increasingly less sense even to talk about a publishing industry, because the core problem publishing solves—the incredible difficulty, complexity, and expense of making something available to the public—has stopped being a problem.

Society doesn’t need newspapers, what we need is journalism and society doesn’t need gatekeepers, it needs search.

**Commerce in a Post-Corporation World**

The ‘Pay What You Want’ model radiohead in rainbows 2007

> “I like the people at our record company, but the time is at hand when you have to ask why anyone needs one… And, yes, it probably would give us perverse pleasure to say ‘fuck you’ to this decaying business model.”

*Thom Yorke, 2003 after Radiohead’s contract with EMI/Capitol expired*

The established business model worked in an analogue world because production wasn’t accessible to everybody and it was expensive.

In exchange for a large percentage of sales, the record companies not only advanced hefty sums of money for recording but also took care of distribution and (to varying degrees) marketing.

But user generated content and any other internet viral prove that thanks to digital media, marketing is in the hands of the people. Why pay for production and marketing when you can do it yourself?

In today’s digital world, artists have at their disposal affordable professional production tools and the internet, which provides marketing, sales, and distribution. Producers make their content available on the internet in hopes it will find an audience willing to support it.

Bands make their music free. The hope is that loyal fans will pay for premium releases, attend concerts and buy merchandise. In turn consumers become marketers because they spread through word-of-mouth their interest in the product to friends and acquaintances.

Radiohead’s music already is widely available on peer-to-peer sites, yet the band still sells millions of albums. With In Rainbows, which was released in October 2007 on Radiohead’s Web site, the band let fans decide what they want to pay.

In addition to the pay-what-you-want download, Radiohead sold In Rainbows as a £40 special-edition boxed set, which included the album on CD, two vinyl records, a CD with additional songs, photos, artwork, and lyrics.

Radiohead made approximately the same from the pay-what-you-want model sale than they did from their previous album’s conventionally distributed method. People paid less, but the band got a bigger cut.

David Bowie forecast change in the music industry in a June 2002 New York Times article:

> “The absolute transformation of everything that we ever thought about music will take place within the next ten years, and nothing is going to be able to stop it… I see absolutely no point in pretending that it’s not going to happen. Music itself is going to become like running water or electricity.”

There is no reason why this should only apply to music. However the model only works for famous artists who can make an impact. People simply won’t pay for something that they can get for free and artists will not make as much money.
