## Overview

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Goal" desc="Per-application network control on Linux: see who's using the connection, how much data they're moving, and block any of them with a single command." />
  <KV term="Inspiration" desc="TripMode, a macOS/Windows-only app that does exactly this. Nothing equivalent existed for Linux, so I built one." />
</KVGroup>

## Architecture

Travel Mode is written in Rust and split into three parts: a background service that runs as root, a command-line tool, and a desktop app. Only the service touches the network. The other two just ask it questions and send it requests, so there's one source of truth and nothing to fall out of sync.

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="The service" desc="Owns everything: which network you're on, how much each connection is moving, which app owns it, the rules, and the firewall." />
  <KV term="The CLI" desc="Status, live usage, and block or allow, with JSON output for scripting." />
  <KV term="The desktop app" desc="A GNOME-style app with a tray icon. It shows what the service reports and has no networking logic of its own." />
</KVGroup>

Blocking happens in the Linux firewall. Each new outbound connection is held for a moment while the service works out which app it came from, then dropped if that app is blocked.

Everything is designed to fail open: if filtering can't be set up, traffic just flows and the app says filtering is off. A tool that sits between you and the internet has to earn trust, and the worst outcome is it silently breaking your connection while you're tethered somewhere.

## Development highlights

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Kill-on-block" desc="The first version only stopped new connections, so a download already running kept going after you blocked it. Now blocking also cuts the app's open connections, which is what people actually expect the switch to mean." />
  <KV term="Best-effort attribution" desc="Traffic is matched to an app by looking at which process owns each connection. Anything that can't be matched shows as unknown and is left alone, rather than risk blocking the wrong thing." />
  <KV term="Switches that stay put" desc="A slow reply from the service could flick a toggle back mid-click. The app now holds your choice until the service confirms it." />
  <KV term="Chasing a rendering bug" desc="Stray bits of text from other pages kept appearing on screen. I traced it to a bug in the graphics toolkit's renderer on my hardware, not my code, and worked around it." />
</KVGroup>

## Result & impact

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="In daily use" desc="Phase 2 (daemon, CLI, and GUI end to end) is complete and is what I actually run day to day on tethered and metered connections." />
  <KV term="What's next" desc="Usage history and per-app data limits, then automatic profile switching so home Wi-Fi and a phone hotspot can carry different rule sets without touching anything by hand." />
</KVGroup>

The bigger draw was the systems side rather than the UI: designing something that sits directly in the Linux firewall, has to reason correctly about connections the kernel is tracking and what happens under every failure, and still stays boring and predictable enough to trust running as root on a machine I use every day.
