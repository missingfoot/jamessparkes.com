## Overview

QD Design manufactured and sold Chinese antique-inspired revival furniture and imported antique Chinese homeware, decorations, and furniture through a site built years earlier as a classic ASP (VBScript) catalogue with a custom-coded admin tool bolted on the side. It could display products and take enquiries, but it could not sell anything. There was no cart, checkout, or basket anywhere in the site. I wanted to design and build its replacement and went with a WordPress site with WooCommerce stack (popular and easy to work with at the time) capable of running the retail operation the old one couldn't.

## Project: migrating a bespoke catalogue into WooCommerce

### Problem

The existing site was a working catalogue but a dead end for growth. It ran entirely in classic ASP, with every product edit going through pages like product_add.asp, product_edit.asp and product_delete.asp, a hand-built CMS with none of the plugin ecosystem, payment integrations, or security patching a modern platform gets for free. Customers could browse products, request a brochure, or apply for a trade account, but every purchase still happened off-site, by phone or through a trade relationship. That capped how much revenue the site itself could generate, and how easily the client could add or change products without calling a developer.

### Goals

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Real ecommerce" desc="Give customers the ability to actually buy on the site (cart, checkout, and payment) for the first time." />
  <KV term="Self-service content" desc="Move product and page management off a bespoke admin tool and onto a CMS the client's team could run themselves." />
  <KV term="Preserve what worked" desc="Carry over the trade and wholesale pricing model that mattered to the business, without rebuilding it as another one-off system." />
  <KV term="Don't over-build" desc="Solve the same business problems with a proven, extensible open-source stack rather than a second bespoke platform." />
</KVGroup>

### Constraints & considerations

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Legacy data" desc="Years of product, category, and page content existed only inside the old ASP/SQL structure and had to be carried across, not re-entered from scratch." />
  <KV term="Small business budget" desc="No scope for another custom-built checkout or admin system; the solution had to lean on off-the-shelf tooling to stay affordable and maintainable long-term." />
  <KV term="Non-technical maintenance" desc="Whatever replaced the ASP admin panel needed to be usable by the client directly, with no developer in the loop for day-to-day updates." />
</KVGroup>

### Approach & decisions

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Platform choice" desc="Chose WordPress with WooCommerce over a custom build specifically because it came with a real ecosystem: page building, SEO, security, product import tooling, payment gateways, none of which existed in the original bespoke admin." />
  <KV term="Bulk product migration" desc="Rather than re-entering the catalogue by hand, used CSV and XML import tooling to bring the existing product data across from the old system in bulk, keeping the migration accurate and fast." />
  <KV term="Custom theme, not an off-the-shelf template" desc="Built a bespoke child theme on Twenty Twelve rather than a generic WooCommerce theme, with custom template overrides for product and archive pages, so the storefront kept its own identity instead of looking like every other small WooCommerce store." />
  <KV term="Search & discovery" desc="Added relevance-based product search, something the original site's search page never really delivered against a growing catalogue." />
  <KV term="Operational tooling over bespoke code" desc="Filled out the rest of the stack with focused, well-supported plugins rather than custom development: security hardening for a site now handling payments, automated backups, analytics, and dynamic pricing for the trade side of the business." />
</KVGroup>

### The stack

This is the plugin stack I settled on to cover the gaps left by the old bespoke admin panel, without building anything custom:

- WooCommerce
- WP All Import
- Relevanssi
- Dynamic Pricing
- Better WP Security
- BackupBuddy

### Outcome & impact

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="First real online sales channel" desc="The client went from a site that could only display products and take enquiries to one that could take a payment end to end: cart, checkout, and order management, all inside WooCommerce." />
  <KV term="Independent content management" desc="Product and page updates moved from a bespoke admin panel only a developer could safely extend, to a dashboard the client's own staff could manage day to day." />
  <KV term="Retained trade pricing model" desc="The wholesale pricing behaviour that mattered to the business carried through into the new platform, instead of being lost in the migration." />
  <KV term="Reduced technical risk" desc="Moving off unsupported classic ASP and a single-purpose admin tool onto a platform with an active plugin ecosystem meant the client was no longer solely dependent on one developer to keep the site running or safe." />
  <KV term="Direct-to-consumer growth" desc="The number of customers buying directly from the site went up dramatically, with revenue increasing more than 3x. The company no longer had to rely on wholesalers, stockists, and flash-sale reseller sites, and could make more profit per item sold." />
  <KV term="Marketing investment" desc="The business made enough money to hire a dedicated marketing person, who kicked off B2C sales with advertising that grew regular customer numbers by 10x." />
</KVGroup>

### Reflections & key takeaways

The hardest part wasn't moving the products across technically; it was deciding what from the old bespoke system was actually worth preserving, like trade pricing and category structure, versus what was only there because it had to be hand-built from scratch, like the entire admin panel.

For a small retail client, the win wasn't building something more custom than the original ASP site; it was replacing bespoke, single-developer infrastructure with an open, well-supported platform that could do more, more safely, for less ongoing cost. Introducing a cart and checkout wasn't just a technical feature addition; it was the first time the client's website could generate revenue directly, rather than existing purely as a digital brochure. And by moving to WooCommerce, the client picked up a platform that could keep growing through plugins and themes, rather than needing another full rebuild the next time the business's needs changed.

### How I worked with the factory

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Built on the factory's own specs" desc="The original cabinets in the range were specced by the factory itself, and I used their detailed spec sheets as the reference for my designs, so new pieces matched the rest of the range in construction and proportion. My plans could be simpler than theirs: the factory already knew its own construction details, so each sheet gave the dimensions and layout it needed rather than every joint." />
  <KV term="Designed around the patterns" desc="Many pieces carried painted patterns, so I designed the panels and proportions with the patterns in mind and placed them where they would sit best on each piece, inside and out." />
  <KV term="Spec sheets that answer the questions" desc="Bespoke pieces went out with a spec sheet listing the size, colours and patterns, hardware and any extras, so the factory had everything it needed to price and build it without a round of questions." />
  <KV term="A naming convention" desc="I named every design to a consistent convention covering its collection, type, size and finish, so any piece could be coded, looked up and referenced the same way by us and the factory." />

## Project: a cabinet that comes apart for tight stairs

<InlineVideo src="/videos/qd-knockdown-cabinet.mp4" poster={knockdownPoster} alt="A tall turquoise Chinese wedding cabinet separating into its top, doors, shelves, carcass and drawer base, then going back together into one solid piece" class="video-capped" />

### Problem

QD's cabinets were built in the factory as solid, fully assembled pieces, which is a big part of why they feel like real furniture. That was a problem for customers whose homes couldn't take a tall assembled cabinet: flats up very small, awkward staircases where the piece physically wouldn't make the turns. Those customers were asking for a version that could be broken down for the trip up the stairs, but none of the designs allowed for it.

### Goals

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Breaks down to fit" desc="Split the cabinet into sections small enough to carry up narrow, awkward stairs where the assembled piece couldn't go." />
  <KV term="Solid once rebuilt" desc="Once reassembled it had to be as strong and rigid as the standard cabinet, not wobbly the way flat-pack furniture is." />
  <KV term="Seamless finish" desc="Back together, it should look like one solid cabinet again, with no visible sign it had ever come apart." />
  <KV term="Buildable by the factory" desc="The design had to fit the way the factory in China already built these pieces, not need a new production process." />
</KVGroup>

### Approach & decisions

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Designed in SketchUp" desc="Modelled the knockdown version in SketchUp, drawn to the factory's own standards so they could build straight from the drawings." />
  <KV term="Sections, not flat-pack panels" desc="Rather than breaking it down into loose flat boards, the cabinet separates into a few rigid sections: the top, the doors, the shelves, the main carcass and the drawer base. Each one keeps its own strength, and there are far fewer joints to go wobbly." />
  <KV term="Assembled by the company, not the customer" desc="The rebuild was done in the customer's flat by QD's own delivery team, so the joints could be made properly tight instead of designed around what a customer could manage with an Allen key." />
</KVGroup>

### Outcome & impact

<KVGroup>
  <KV term="Adopted by the factory" desc="The factory used the design for a whole range of pieces whenever a customer asked for the breakable version." />
  <KV term="Sales that would have been lost" desc="Customers who couldn't have got a standard cabinet into their home could now buy one, and end up with the same solid, seamless piece as everyone else." />
</KVGroup>
